
# CMB-6544PF Electret Condenser Microphone — Datasheet Answers

> **Context:** This worksheet uses datasheet reading as a core skill. While the ATmega328PB datasheet serves as the classic teaching example, the same systematic approach applies to any component — here the CMB-6544PF microphone and RMCF0603FT2K20 resistor. The goal is to locate, interpret, and reason about specifications, not just copy numbers.

| Parameter                             | Conditions                                     | Max | Unit |

**Where in the datasheet:** Page 1, Specifications table, row "current consumption (I<sub>DSS</sub>)".

**Practical implication:** At 0.5 mA max, this microphone draws very little current — suitable for battery-powered and microcontroller-based designs (e.g., powered from an ATmega328PB GPIO pin, which can source up to 40 mA per pin — far more than needed).

## (R5) If You Change the Input Voltage, Will This Current Consumption Remain the Same?

**Short answer: No, not exactly the same — but it will remain relatively stable across the operating voltage range due to the FET's constant-current behaviour.**

### Reasoning

1. **What the datasheet tells us directly:**

   - Operating voltage range: **4.5 Vdc (min) to 10 Vdc (max)** — the device is designed to work across this entire span.
   - Current consumption is specified at only **one point**: V<sub>S</sub> = 4.5 Vdc, R<sub>L</sub> = 1 KΩ → max 0.5 mA.
   - Sensitivity reduction (S-V<sub>S</sub>): when V<sub>S</sub> drops from 4.5 V to 1.5 V, sensitivity changes by at most -3 dB — a small degradation, indicating the internal bias point is stable over a wide voltage range.
2. **What the circuit topology tells us (page 1, Measurement Circuit):**

   - The CMB-6544PF is an **Electret Condenser Microphone (ECM)** with a built-in **FET (Field-Effect Transistor) impedance converter**.
   - The FET is configured as a common-source amplifier / constant-current sink. In this configuration, the FET's drain current (I<sub>DSS</sub>) is primarily determined by the FET's own pinch-off characteristics, **not** by the external supply voltage (as long as V<sub>S</sub> stays above the FET's saturation voltage).
   - This is fundamentally different from a resistor, where current scales linearly with voltage (Ohm's law: I = V/R).
3. **Conclusion:**

   - Across the specified operating range (4.5 V – 10 V), the current will stay **close to ~0.5 mA** because the FET regulates it.
   - It will **not** be perfectly constant — real FETs have a finite output impedance, so a small increase in current with increasing voltage is expected.
   - If you drop below 4.5 V (e.g., to 1.5 V), the FET may begin to fall out of saturation, and the sensitivity reduction spec (-3 dB max) suggests the circuit still functions but with degraded performance — the current would likely decrease.

> **Key takeaway:** The datasheet does not spell this out explicitly — it requires understanding *how* an electret microphone's internal FET stage works. The specification table gives you the data points; the schematic and your knowledge of FET behaviour let you interpolate between them.

| --------- | -------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| RMCF0603  | **0.1 W**      | 75 V                | 150 V                | ±200 ppm/°C (1%), ±250 ppm/°C (5%) |

**Where in the datasheet:** RMCF/RMCP Series datasheet, Electrical Specifications — RMCF table, row "RMCF0603".

### Resistor Marking Breakdown

| Code Segment   | Meaning                                                      |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **RMCF** | Series: standard-power thick film chip resistor              |
| **0603** | Package: 0603 imperial (0.063" × 0.031" / 1.6 mm × 0.8 mm) |
| **F**    | Tolerance: ±1%                                              |
| **T**    | Packaging: 7" tape & reel                                    |
| **2K20** | Resistance: 2.2 kΩ (2,200 Ω)                               |

### Derating Curve

The RMCF/RMCP series datasheet states: *"Power derating from 100% at 70°C to zero at +155°C"*.

This means:

- At **70°C and below**: the resistor can dissipate its **full rated power** — **0.1 W**.
- From **70°C to 155°C**: the allowable power dissipation decreases **linearly** to 0 W.
- At **155°C**: the resistor cannot dissipate any power — it can only survive at this temperature with no load.

**For our resistor at 70°C:** The answer is **0.1 W** — exactly the rated power, with no derating applied.

### Why This Matters

If your circuit operates at an ambient temperature above 70°C (e.g., inside an enclosure near a power regulator), you must derate the resistor. For example, at 112.5°C (the midpoint of the derating range), the RMCF0603 can only handle 0.05 W. Choosing a higher-power part (e.g., RMCP0603 at 0.25 W, or stepping up to an 0805 package) would be necessary in such cases.

## (R7) DC Current per I/O Pin & Smallest Resistive Load at 5V

### Limiting DC Current

| Parameter                              | Limit           | Unit |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------- | ---- |
| DC Current per I/O Pin                 | **40.0**  | mA   |
| DC Current V<sub>CC</sub> and GND Pins | **200.0** | mA   |

**Where in the datasheet:** Section 33.1, "Absolute Maximum Ratings" table.

**How to read it:** These are *absolute maximum ratings* — stress limits beyond which permanent damage may occur. Each individual I/O pin can source (drive high) or sink (drive low) up to **40 mA**. The total current through all I/O pins combined must not exceed 200 mA on the V<sub>CC</sub> or GND rails. This is not a recommended operating condition — it is the damage threshold.

### Smallest Resistive Load at 5V That Exceeds the Limit

Given:

- V<sub>CC</sub> (high-level output voltage when driving high) ≈ 5V
- I<sub>limit</sub> = 40 mA

Using Ohm's Law:

$$
R_{\text{min}} = \frac{V}{I_{\text{limit}}} = \frac{5\text{V}}{40\text{mA}} = \frac{5}{0.04} = 125\text{ }\Omega
$$

**Answer: Any resistive load smaller than 125 Ω will draw more than 40 mA and exceed the rated limiting current.**

| Load Resistance | Current Drawn (I = V/R) | Exceeds 40 mA?                     |
| --------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 150 Ω          | 33.3 mA                 | No — safe                         |
| 125 Ω          | 40.0 mA                 | At the limit                       |
| 100 Ω          | 50.0 mA                 | **Yes — exceeds limit**     |
| 50 Ω           | 100.0 mA                | **Yes — well beyond limit** |

**Important nuance:** In practice, the output voltage will droop under load. The datasheet specifies V<sub>OH</sub> (output high voltage) at I<sub>OH</sub> = -20 mA as 4.2V minimum at 85°C and V<sub>CC</sub> = 5V. At 40 mA the voltage drop would be larger, so the actual current with a 125 Ω load would be somewhat less than 40 mA. A more conservative calculation would use the guaranteed V<sub>OH</sub> at the target current. However, for the purpose of identifying the *limiting* resistance, 125 Ω is the correct theoretical threshold — any lower resistance risks exceeding the absolute maximum rating.

## (R8) I/O Pin Leakage Current

### Specification

| Symbol         | Parameter                        | Condition                       | Max         | Unit |
| -------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------- | ---- |
| I<sub>IL</sub> | Input Leakage Current (pin low)  | V<sub>CC</sub> = 5.5V, pin low  | **1** | µA  |
| I<sub>IH</sub> | Input Leakage Current (pin high) | V<sub>CC</sub> = 5.5V, pin high | **1** | µA  |

**Where in the datasheet:** Section 33.2, DC Characteristics table, rows "I<sub>IL</sub>" and "I<sub>IH</sub>".

### What Is Leakage Current?

**Leakage current** is the very small current that flows into or out of an I/O pin when it is configured as an **input** (high-impedance / tri-state / Hi-Z mode). Ideally, an input pin would draw zero current — it would have infinite input impedance. In reality, the MOSFET transistors that form the input buffer have **non-ideal** behaviour:

1. **Subthreshold conduction:** Even when a MOSFET is "off," a tiny current flows between drain and source.
2. **Gate oxide leakage:** The ultra-thin gate oxide of modern CMOS transistors allows a minute tunnelling current.
3. **ESD protection diodes:** The on-chip protection diodes have reverse-bias leakage.

These effects combine to produce a leakage current typically in the **nanoamp to microamp** range. The ATmega328PB guarantees **≤ 1 µA** at the worst-case condition (V<sub>CC</sub> = 5.5V, extreme temperature).

**Why it matters:**

- When reading a high-impedance sensor or a voltage divider with large resistor values, leakage current can cause a measurable voltage error.
- When an input pin is left **floating** (unconnected), leakage current can cause the pin voltage to drift to an indeterminate logic level, leading to unpredictable behaviour and increased power consumption (oscillating input buffers).
- This is why unused pins should be tied to a defined logic level (V<sub>CC</sub> or GND) via an external pull-up/pull-down resistor, or by enabling the internal pull-up.

**Comparison with output mode:** When the pin is configured as an output, leakage current is irrelevant — the pin is actively driven. The relevant limit in output mode is the 40 mA DC current limit (R7 above).

# Blue LED (FD-5TB-1) — Current-Limiting Resistor Calculation

> **Source:** Shenzhen Fedy Technology Co., Ltd — FD-5TB-1 Blue LED Datasheet (5 mm, InGaN, water-clear lens).

---

## (R9) Smallest Current-Limiting Resistor at 5V DC

### Key Specifications from the Datasheet

| Parameter                                | Symbol              | Min           | Typ | Max           | Unit |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------- | --- | ------------- | ---- |
| Forward Voltage                          | V<sub>F</sub>       | **3.0** | 3.2 | **3.4** | V    |
| Forward Current (continuous)             | I<sub>F</sub>       | —            | —  | **20**  | mA   |
| Peak Forward Current (1/10 duty @ 1 kHz) | I<sub>F(Peak)</sub> | —            | —  | 50            | mA   |
| Reverse Voltage                          | V<sub>R</sub>       | —            | —  | 5             | V    |
| Power Dissipation                        | P<sub>d</sub>       | —            | —  | 100           | mW   |

**Where in the datasheet:** Page 2, "Absolute Maximum Ratings" and "Electronic Optical Characteristics" tables.

### Which V<sub>F</sub> Defines the Worst Case?

The current through the LED and resistor is given by Ohm's Law:

$$
I = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_F}{R}
$$

For a **fixed resistor value**, the current is **highest** when the voltage drop across the resistor is **largest** — i.e., when V<sub>F</sub> is at its **minimum**.

| V<sub>F</sub> Case                   | Voltage Across Resistor      | Current Through LED             |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| V<sub>F</sub> = **3.0V (min)** | 5.0V − 3.0V =**2.0V** | **Highest** ← worst case |
| V<sub>F</sub> = 3.2V (typ)           | 5.0V − 3.2V = 1.8V          | Medium                          |
| V<sub>F</sub> = 3.4V (max)           | 5.0V − 3.4V = 1.6V          | Lowest                          |

**Conclusion:** The **minimum V<sub>F</sub> (3.0V)** is the worst case because it produces the highest current for any given resistor. We must design for this worst case so that the LED current never exceeds its rated maximum, regardless of V<sub>F</sub> variation.

### Calculation

Using the worst-case V<sub>F</sub> = 3.0V and the maximum rated continuous forward current I<sub>F</sub> = 20 mA:

$$
R_{\text{min}} = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{F(\text{min})}}{I_{F(\text{max})}} = \frac{5.0\text{V} - 3.0\text{V}}{20\text{mA}} = \frac{2.0\text{V}}{0.020\text{A}} = 100\text{ }\Omega
$$

### Verification Across All V<sub>F</sub> Cases

Using R = **100 Ω**, the current for each V<sub>F</sub> case is:

| V<sub>F</sub> | Calculation        | Current           | Status               |
| ------------- | ------------------ | ----------------- | -------------------- |
| 3.0V (min)    | (5.0 − 3.0) / 100 | **20.0 mA** | At the limit — safe |
| 3.2V (typ)    | (5.0 − 3.2) / 100 | 18.0 mA           | Safe                 |
| 3.4V (max)    | (5.0 − 3.4) / 100 | 16.0 mA           | Safe                 |

**Answer: 100 Ω**

A 100 Ω resistor ensures that even with the lowest possible forward voltage (3.0V), the LED current does not exceed the 20 mA maximum rating. With higher V<sub>F</sub> values, the current is lower — still within the recommended 5–20 mA operating range noted in the datasheet.

### Practical Considerations

1. **Standard resistor values:** 100 Ω is a standard E12/E24 value, so no rounding is needed.
2. **Resistor power dissipation:** P = I² × R = (0.020)² × 100 = **40 mW** — a standard 0.1W (0603) or 0.125W (0805) resistor is adequate.
3. **Brightness variation:** With V<sub>F</sub> varying from 3.0V to 3.4V, the current varies from 20 mA to 16 mA (±11%). The luminous intensity will vary accordingly, but remains within safe limits.
4. **If you want margin:** Designers often use a slightly higher resistance (e.g., 120 Ω or 150 Ω) to stay comfortably below the 20 mA absolute maximum. The datasheet recommends 5–20 mA as the operating range, so a 150 Ω resistor would give ~13.3 mA (worst case) — still bright, with longer LED life and lower power consumption.
